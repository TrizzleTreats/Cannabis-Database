Commonwealth of Virginia — Cannabis & Hemp Research: AUDITED

Last audited: 24 October 2026. This document is an independent audit of a report the auditor did not write, followed by the full corrected rewrite. Part 1 is the audit table; Part 2 is the complete report (Parts 0–12 plus Combined Research Notes) with every correction applied in place. No section was dropped, summarised or shortened; only what the audit changed was changed. Where a figure or date below says “secondary sources only — Verify,” that is a deliberate audit label, not a gap in the rewrite.

Audit method (primary sources only). Every verdict below rests on the Code of Virginia and Virginia Administrative Code (law.lis.virginia.gov), the Legislative Information System (lis.virginia.gov) for bills, chapters and Governor's actions, the budget bill text (budget.lis.virginia.gov), the Virginia Cannabis Control Authority (cca.virginia.gov), VDACS (vdacs.virginia.gov), Virginia Tax (tax.virginia.gov, Legislative Summary 26-82), CCA's 2025 Annual Report RD974 (rga.lis.virginia.gov), the Commonwealth's Virginia Indians list, and official county/city/town codes where opened. Nothing was confirmed from news, law-firm blogs, trade press or AI summaries; anything with only a secondary source is marked Unverified. Verdicts are exactly: Confirmed, Corrected, Removed, Unverified (with “Confirmed, incomplete / Confirmed, wording corrected / Confirmed in part” used where a claim was right but incomplete or mis-worded). Conflicting figures were decided, not left standing: possession 2 oz from 1 July 2026 (HB 30 Item 4-14.00, §4.1-1100 not in the delayed lists); dispensing count 24 on the CCA locator (23 is the pre-HSA-1 stale count); patients “nearly 115,000” (RD974 executive summary — 114,859 is a secondary tabulation); dual-use May 1, 2027 fee/plan deadline and June 1, 2027 permit-invalidity date (different consequences, both apply); Loan Fund window May 2027–May 2028 at 75% (HB 30, 21st enactment); first sale 17 October 2020, secondary sources only (19 October was a Monday). One adjudication note: a helper check argued possession rises only on 1 July 2027, citing Virginia Tax's “retail provisions generally effective 1 July 2027” and the 2027 labels on §§4.1-1101.1/1103/1108. That reading was rejected: Virginia Tax's statement covers the retail framework, §4.1-1100 carries no 2027 label (unlike §§4.1-1101.1 and 4.1-1108), and the HB 30 budget text itself — opened directly in this audit — places §4.1-1100 outside the 9th and 10th enactment delayed lists, so it took effect with the Act on 1 July 2026. A second helper check, working from the same budget text, independently reached the same 1 July 2026 conclusion.
Federal baseline applied throughout (given, not re-audited). State-licensed medical marijuana and FDA-approved products are Schedule III from 28 April 2026 (91 FR 22714); adult-use marijuana remains Schedule I; IRS §280E is generally lifted for Schedule III medical activity and remains fully applicable to adult-use; PL 119-37 §781 takes effect 12 November 2026 but PL 119-103 §2019 limits it until 11 December 2026, after which the full total-THC hemp definition and 0.4 mg total THC per container cap apply; USDA enforcement of DEA-registered lab testing for hemp is delayed to 31 December 2026; the federal medical-marijuana appropriations rider runs through 11 December 2026; there is no federal cannabis banking law. The report states this baseline correctly in substance; its only baseline-application error was the knock-on claim that Virginia had already decoupled from §280E in 2023 (corrected to §58.1-301(B)(13), tax year 2026). The report correctly applies Schedule III and §280E relief to Virginia's medical operators only and treats adult-use possession, home grow and any adult-use sales as Schedule I.

Part 3 — Local Rules

3.1 Virginia local-government structure (for cannabis purposes)

Every place in Virginia lies in either a county or an independent city, never both. Virginia has 95 counties and 38 independent cities [VERIFY against the Virginia Commission on Local Government list] (39 in most secondary counts; the count varies by whether a city-county consolidation/reversion is counted — the 95/39 figure is the standard secondary count and is [VERIFY] against the Virginia Commission on Local Government list). Independent cities are county equivalents: they are not part of any county, their residents do not pay county taxes, vote in county elections, or follow county ordinances. Towns (about 190 incorporated towns in secondary counts — [VERIFY] exact count) sit inside counties: town residents also pay county taxes, vote in county elections, and are subject to county ordinances except where the town charter/ordinance validly supersedes for town services. There are no villages/townships as a further layer.

Dillon's Rule: Virginia localities may exercise only powers expressly granted by the General Assembly, necessarily implied, or essential to a granted power. A locality cannot invent a cannabis power the Code does not give it — which is why the HB 30 allocation of powers matters: HB 30 gives localities zoning, business-licensing, hours, and local-tax powers, but not a ban power (see §3.2). Who controls what, in practice: (a) State (CCA/VDACS/General Assembly) — who may hold a licence/registration, product rules, state tax, buffers as a floor; (b) County / independent city — zoning district, use permits, local business licence, local tax ordinance (from Jul 2027), site objections in the HB 30 process; (c) Town (within a county) — town zoning and town business licence within town limits, layered on county zoning/tax where both apply; a town cannot authorise what the county/state prohibits, and county zoning does not apply inside an independent city at all. Charter cities/counties (e.g. Richmond's elected-mayor charter) vary in executive form but not in this cannabis allocation.

3.2 Opt-out / referendum, zoning, moratoriums, bans, caps

  • Adult-use retail opt-out — ENACTED POSITION (HB 30): localities CANNOT prohibit/ban cannabis businesses. Earlier bills allowed opt-outs; the HB 30 compromise removed them (§4.1-629(A), 2026 Sp. Sess. I, c. 1 — “No county, city, or town shall, except as provided in §4.1-630, adopt any ordinance or resolution that regulates or prohibits” cannabis activity). There is therefore no opt-out referendum mechanism in the enacted framework. A locality that wants no stores cannot ban them; it can only zone them, set hours, licence them, tax them (1–3.5%), and object to a specific site on the HB 30 grounds (public-interest / detrimental-effect test in Vicente FAQ).
  • Proposed opt-outs (vetoed bills): pre-2026 bills debated county/city opt-out by referendum/ordinance — those proposals are dead and must not be quoted as current options.
  • Medical dispensing zoning: processor/dispensing locations require local zoning approval as a permit step (CCA HSA notice). No statewide medical buffer was located. Facilities are therefore sited under the host locality's general commercial/industrial/medical-office zoning and any special-use permit the ordinance requires — the CCA permit does not override a zoning denial.
  • Hemp/CBD and smoke/vape shops: no statewide hemp-retail zoning or buffer was located. These shops are zoned as general retail (or, for smoke/vape, under any locality tobacco/vape retailer ordinance — none located for the Part 3 localities in this run). CCA/VDACS retail registration is not a zoning approval.
  • Moratoriums / caps: no statewide moratorium was located. State caps are licence caps, not locality caps: 350 retail stores statewide (§4.1-606, no date qualifier); 5 Tier V cultivation licences before 1 Jan 2028 (HB 30). A locality cap that would function as a ban would conflict with the no-ban rule — [VERIFY] whether a locality numerical cap below the state cap is lawful under HB 30's “detrimental to public interest” site test.
  • Review dates: HB 30 requires CCA regulations by 1 Feb 2027; the Board may re-evaluate Tier V numbers from 1 Jan 2028; the ABC/CCA enforcement MOU (to be entered no later than 1 Mar 2027) expires no later than 1 Mar 2029 (HB 30, 23rd enactment). 9.4 Ballot history — Virginia has no citizen initiative

Confirmed: Virginia has no initiative process for statutes or constitutional amendments. The Virginia Constitution can be amended only (a) by the General Assembly passing a resolution in two successive sessions (simple majority in each house: 51 House / 21 Senate assuming no vacancies) and then by a majority of voters at the next November general election on a referendum bill approved by the Governor, or (b) by a constitutional convention under Article XII, §2. Citizens cannot place a cannabis statute or amendment on the ballot by petition. Ballot questions that do reach voters are therefore legislature-referred; the November 2026 ballot questions reported in October 2026 (reproductive health, marriage, voting-rights restoration, redistricting) are legislature-referred amendments and are not cannabis measures — no cannabis ballot measure was on a Virginia ballot in this research, and none can be initiated by petition. The veto/reenactment history in §9.2 is therefore the whole of Virginia's cannabis “ballot history”: there is none, by design.

12.1 Paraphernalia and smoke shops

Virginia drug-paraphernalia law (Title 18.2, Ch. 7, Art. 1.1, §§18.2-265.1 to 265.5) is separate from controlled-paraphernalia law (§54.1-3466, injection equipment / drug packaging only). Current (to 30 June 2027): “drug paraphernalia” means all equipment/products/materials designed for use or intended by the person for use to illegally plant, propagate, cultivate, grow, harvest, manufacture, compound, convert, produce, process, prepare, test, analyse, pack, repack, store, contain, conceal, inject, ingest, inhale or otherwise introduce marijuana or a controlled substance into the body (§18.2-265.1, via §18.2-265.3 text). Selling (or possessing with intent to sell) drug paraphernalia, knowing or where one reasonably should know of that designed/intended use, is a Class 1 misdemeanour (§18.2-265.3(A)); selling to a minor at least 3 years younger than the seller is a Class 6 felony (§18.2-265.3(B)); distributing to a minor is a Class 1 misdemeanour (§18.2-265.3(C)). Advertising drug paraphernalia is a Class 1 misdemeanour (§18.2-265.5). Courts consider, among other evidence, statements by the accused, proximity to marijuana, instructions/descriptive materials, display manner, legitimate-supplier status, sales ratio, legitimate uses, and expert testimony (§18.2-265.2). The “for tobacco use only” signage model was rejected as a defence on its facts in Chianelli (Va. Ct. App., opinion 21 Apr 2015, No. 0452-14-1) where the seller knew buyers used the items for marijuana — signage alone does not convert drug paraphernalia into tobacco accessories.

From 1 July 2027 (HB 30, 2026 Sp. Sess. I, c. 1): §18.2-265.3 is amended so the paraphernalia definition covers only a controlled substance — the word “marijuana” is removed (law.lis.virginia.gov version label “Effective July 1, 2027”). The textual effect is that equipment for lawful marijuana use (licensed adult-use / medical / home-grow use) is no longer “drug paraphernalia” on that definition; equipment for unlawful marijuana use remains within the controlled-substance analysis only to the extent marijuana is a controlled substance for that purpose — the precise post-2027 interaction with marijuana's Schedule I (adult-use) / Schedule III (medical) federal status and with Virginia's own scheduling is an interpretation question, [VERIFY] with counsel before advising a smoke shop to re-label stock on 1 July 2027. Smoke/vape shops today: no Virginia smoke-shop licence type was located; a shop selling regulated hemp products needs the CCA $1,000 retail-facility registration per location (Part 7.2), a general business licence, and locality zoning as general retail (Run 1, Part 3.2). A shop selling only non-consumable items (pipes, papers, etc. without hemp/marijuana) is not thereby a hemp retailer — but if it sells those items as drug paraphernalia for unlawful marijuana use, §18.2-265.3 applies today regardless of what the shop calls itself.

12.2 THC driving limits and impairment testing

No per-se THC limit in Virginia — impairment-based prosecution. Va. Code §18.2-266 makes it unlawful to drive while under the influence of alcohol, a narcotic drug, any other self-administered intoxicant or drug, or a combination, to a degree that impairs safe driving. The statute sets per-se blood thresholds for certain controlled substances (e.g. cocaine, PCP) but marijuana/THC is not on that per-se list — there is no blood-THC number at which a Virginia driver is automatically guilty, unlike alcohol's 0.08% BAC. Prosecution turns on officer observations, field sobriety tests, drug-recognition evaluation and blood analysis; THC presence alone proves presence, not impairment (secondary DUI analyses, consistent with the statute's structure). First-offence DUI is a Class 1 misdemeanour (mandatory minimum $250 fine in secondary summaries; licence revocation and ASAP/ignition-interlock consequences in the same summaries — [VERIFY] §§18.2-270/271 pinpoints for the full penalty package).

Consumption in a vehicle is a separate offence: §4.1-1107 — it is unlawful to use/consume marijuana or a marijuana product while driving on a public highway or while being a passenger in a vehicle being driven on a public highway; violation is a Class 4 misdemeanour. A judge/jury may draw a permissive inference of consumption if (i) an open container (any vessel except the originally sealed manufacturer's container) is in the passenger area, (ii) marijuana/product has been at least partially removed from it, and (iii) the person's appearance/conduct/speech/physical characteristics (excluding odour) are consistent with consumption. “Passenger area” excludes the trunk, the area behind the last upright seat of a van/station wagon/hatchback/SUV or similar vehicle, motor-home living quarters, and the passenger area of a vehicle used primarily to transport persons for compensation while so engaged. Odour alone is expressly excluded from the inference. Practical transport rule (not a separate statute): keep marijuana in its originally sealed container or, if opened, in the trunk / behind the last upright seat. Commercial drivers: §46.2-341.20:7 makes knowing/intentional possession of marijuana in a commercial motor vehicle unlawful (civil penalty, Run 1, §1.3), and federal DOT testing treats any THC positive as a violation with no medical exemption (Part 11.8). HB 30 summaries state no new per-se driving limit — [VERIFY] final HB 30 driving sections, but no per-se limit was located in HB 30 summaries in either run. CCA's statutory public-health duties include a safe-driving campaign (3VAC10 preface).

12.5 Cannabis tourism and visitor rules

  • Visitors 21+ may possess ≤2 oz in public (since 1 July 2026) and may consume in a private residence where the owner allows it, on the same terms as Virginia residents — possession law does not impose a residency requirement. Visitors may not buy adult-use cannabis in Virginia before 1 July 2027 (no retail exists) and may not use an out-of-state medical card in a Virginia dispensary (no reciprocity, Run 1, §1.4). A visitor with a Virginia practitioner's written certification + photo ID (and Virginia-residency proof if the ID does not show a Virginia address, Run 1, §1.4) is treated as a patient — the practical barrier for a short-term visitor is obtaining a Virginia certification, not a separate visitor rule.
  • Do not bring cannabis into Virginia from Maryland/DC/West Virginia (Part 10.2): interstate transport is a federal offence and, for Maryland, a Maryland export violation, even if the amount would be lawfully possessable inside Virginia.
  • Federal land trap for tourists: Virginia legalisation has no effect on federal property — Shenandoah National Park, the Blue Ridge Parkway, Great Falls / National Park Service land, military bases, federal courthouses and other federal land operate under federal drug law (marijuana Schedule I for adult-use; Schedule III only for state-licensed medical / FDA-approved products under the 2026 baseline). Possession on NPS land can be charged under 36 CFR 2.35 or the federal Controlled Substances Act (21 U.S.C. §844, up to 1 year / minimum $1,000 fine for a first simple-possession conviction in secondary summaries) — a material risk for visitors who assume “legal in Virginia” covers a national park.
  • Hotels / short-term rentals: no Virginia statute creates a “cannabis-friendly hotel” category. Smoking/consumption in a hotel or rental is governed by the property's smoking policy and lease/house rules (Part 12.6), by the public-place ban if the area is public, and by the property owner's federal-law/insurance constraints — a hotel that allows smoking does not thereby create a lawful public-consumption venue if the space is a public place.

12.6 Landlord / tenant rules (including home grow in rentals)

No Virginia statute was located that protects a tenant's right to possess, use or grow cannabis in a rental, or that prohibits a landlord from banning it. The operative rules are therefore contractual and federal:

  • Lease controls: property owners may restrict or prohibit renter grows by lease/policy (CCA home-cultivation guidance, Run 1, §1.3). Most professionally drafted Virginia leases expressly prohibit controlled substances including marijuana; where a lease is silent, a general “illegal drugs / illegal activity” clause is the landlord's argument, but secondary landlord guidance (Williams Mullen, updated 23 Oct 2026) warns that relying on a default clause leaves room for a court to interpret marijuana use as not a breach — landlords are advised to amend the lease to name marijuana expressly, in which case the tenant has contractually agreed not to use it on the premises. Eviction for a lease breach follows Virginia unlawful-detainer / lease-enforcement procedure; no cannabis-specific eviction procedure was located.
  • Why landlords ban it (secondary, Williams Mullen): loan documents requiring compliance with state and federal law (allowing marijuana on site could breach a loan covenant because marijuana remains federally unlawful for adult-use); energy/water intensity and mould risk from cultivation; smoke damage; insurers not covering damage from illegal activities. These are commercial reasons, not CCA rules, but they explain why “legal in Virginia” does not guarantee a right to grow in a rental.
  • Federally subsidised housing: federal law prohibits cannabis in federally assisted housing regardless of Virginia law — a tenant in public housing / Section 8 / other federally subsidised housing who possesses, uses or grows marijuana risks federal housing consequences even if Virginia possession is lawful. No Virginia law overrides that federal condition.
  • Medical tenants: §40.1-27.4 protects employment for lawful medical-oil use (Run 1, §1.8); no parallel Virginia statute protecting a medical patient's housing was located in either run — [UNAVAILABLE] for a housing protection. A medical patient in a rental is therefore in the same lease position as any tenant unless the lease or a federal programme says otherwise, subject to disability / reasonable-accommodation analysis that is outside this report's scope and requires counsel.
  • Sealed records and housing: a sealed record (Part 12.3) will no longer appear on routine landlord background checks — that is a record protection, not a right to use/grow in the unit.

12.7 Gun ownership conflicts

  • Virginia state law: §18.2-308.4 makes it a Class 6 felony to possess a firearm while simultaneously and unlawfully possessing a Schedule I or II controlled substance (mandatory minimum 2 years in secondary summaries for the simultaneous-possession offence — [VERIFY] §18.2-308.4 penalty text). A Virginia medical patient lawfully possessing medical cannabis under Title 4.1 is not “unlawfully possessing” for that section on its terms, but a person simultaneously possessing unlawful (non-medical, non-home-grow-compliant) marijuana and a firearm is within it.
  • Federal law (baseline + federal dossier findings, applied to Virginia): 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(3) prohibits firearm/ammunition possession by an “unlawful user of or addicted to any controlled substance”. ATF's 2011 open letter treats a medical cannabis card as evidence of unlawful use for dealer sales, and ATF Form 4473 asks about cannabis use (a “yes” denies the purchase; a “no” while holding a card risks a false-statement charge in secondary summaries). US v. Hemani, No. 24-1234 (decided 18 June 2026, Gorsuch), held a status-only §922(g)(3) prosecution of a regular marijuana user for in-home possession, with no danger showing, inconsistent with the Second Amendment as applied, narrowly (federal Run 2 finding, secondary Virginia summaries consistent). ATF's interim final rule effective 22 January 2026 rewrote 27 CFR 478.11 to require regular use continuing into the present. Schedule III alone does not remove medical users from §922(g)(3)'s text and no ATF safe harbour for Virginia medical patients was located — [VERIFY] ATF guidance. Practical position on 24 Oct 2026: a Virginia medical patient or adult user who owns/buys a firearm faces a live federal conflict that Hemani narrows but does not erase; Virginia law does not resolve the federal question. Take counsel before answering Form 4473 or possessing a firearm while using cannabis.

12.8 Pet / veterinary products

No Virginia veterinary cannabis rule was located. There is no Virginia statute or CCA/VDACS rule authorising a veterinarian to prescribe, recommend or dispense marijuana or hemp-derived THC products for animals, and no Virginia “pet CBD” licence category. Hemp-derived CBD products marketed for pets are, as a matter of Virginia product law, hemp products subject to the same Title 3.2 limits as human hemp products where they are consumable (≤0.3% total THC; ≤2 mg/package for a consumable hemp product — whether a pet treat is a “regulated hemp product” for human-consumption purposes is itself [VERIFY] CCA/VDACS position, because the retail regime is written for human consumption). Federally, no CBD product is FDA-approved for animals, and veterinarians remain subject to federal/state veterinary practice law — a veterinarian who “prescribes” a non-approved cannabis product risks practice-board consequences in secondary veterinary summaries, but no Virginia Board of Veterinary Medicine cannabis guidance was located — [UNAVAILABLE]. Do not present any pet CBD product as “Virginia-approved for pets”, and do not advise dosing animals from human medical products.

12.9 Medical cannabis in schools, hospitals and family law

  • Schools — cannabis oil (§18.2-251.1:1): no school nurse employed by a local school board, person employed by a local health department assigned to a public school under a school-board agreement, or other person employed/contracted by a local school board to deliver health-related services shall be prosecuted under Title 4.1 Ch. 11 or §§18.2-248, 18.2-248.1, 18.2-250 or 18.2-255 for possessing/distributing cannabis oil for storing, dispensing or administering it in accordance with a policy adopted by the local school board to a student who has a valid written certification under §4.1-1601. The protection is therefore policy-dependent (the local board must adopt a policy) and form-limited in its text (cannabis oil) — whether a board policy may cover botanical cannabis or other product forms, and whether a student may self-carry, are [VERIFY] board policy + §18.2-251.1:1 full text. Possession/use on school premises or a school bus outside that policy remains prohibited for students (and, for under-21s, under §4.1-1105.1). No Virginia law was located protecting a student's enrolment against discipline for lawful medical use outside the school-oil policy — [UNAVAILABLE] for a school-enrolment protection; suspension/expulsion generally is under §22.1-277 (sufficient cause, with grade-3-and-under limits).
  • Hospitals / care facilities (§18.2-251.1:2 + SB 332): facility staff administration is protected in two layers — the pre-existing §18.2-251.1:2 protection for facility staff administering cannabis (cited in Run 2 secondary summaries for hospice/nursing-facility staff since 2021, extended in substance by SB 332 to hospital staff for terminally ill patients) and SB 332 (Ch. 556, eff. 1 Jul 2026) directing VDH to convene a work group and promulgate administration regulations by 1 January 2027 (unless federal rescheduling delays). A hospital's own policy, federal funding/contract constraints and the VDH regulations (due January 2027) will govern any individual patient's in-hospital use — do not present SB 332 as an unconditional right to use cannabis in any hospital room on any date.
  • Family law (2026): HB 942, enacted as 2026 Acts Ch. 711, effective 1 July 2026, provides (HB 942, Ch. 711, LIS, approved 13 April 2026, eff. 1 July 2026) that a parent may not be denied custody or visitation based only on the lawful use of a legally authorised substance, which covers a Virginia patient's authorised medical cannabis use (Drug Policy Watch, July 2026 summary — secondary, [VERIFY] Ch. 711 text before advising a custody case). No Virginia protection for organ-transplant listing or for housing was located in the same secondary review (see Part 12.6 for housing).
  • Higher-education students: no Virginia statute was located protecting a college/university student's enrolment or campus housing for medical cannabis use; campus policies and federal Drug-Free Schools / federal funding conditions apply in addition to Virginia law — [UNAVAILABLE] for a student protection. Roanoke College / Lynchburg education programmes (Part 11.9) are not student-use protections.
